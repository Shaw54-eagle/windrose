What this is (and deliberately isn't)

Windrose watches. It doesn't trade. That's not a missing feature — it's the design. The first version of this project let a language model place orders on a schedule, and the problem with that isn't that models are dumb; it's that they're confident. A thing that sounds sure of itself, attached to a buy button, is how money disappears politely. So the buy button is gone. Everything on the screen exists to inform a decision that you make, on your broker, on purpose.

The second design rule: every number is deterministic. Not "the AI thinks," not "the model feels" — computed, from price history and reported financials, using formulas you can check by hand. Where a number depends on an assumption (a discount rate, a leverage ratio), the assumption is a slider on the screen, not a value buried in code. If you can't see what's feeding a number, the number is decoration. Nothing here is decoration.

Third rule: the dashboard tells you what is, and occasionally what was. It never tells you what will be. Anything that looks like a forecast — analyst targets, implied growth, Monte Carlo cones — is labeled with exactly how much it doesn't know.

Where the numbers come from, second to second

The pill in the top bar is the heartbeat. Green and pulsing (live · alpaca iex) means a background thread on your machine is asking Alpaca for fresh prices about every two seconds, and the page is repainting every one and a half. Amber (delayed · yfinance) means no Alpaca keys are set, so you're on Yahoo-sourced quotes that lag by minutes. Red means nothing is answering — usually your internet, occasionally a rate limit.

Now the honest part. Alpaca's free feed is IEX — a real exchange, real trades, but a small slice of total US volume, low single digits. In a calm market the last IEX print and the "real" consolidated price are pennies apart. In a fast market they can drift. For watching your own book this is fine. For scalping it is not, and nothing about this dashboard is built for scalping, so we're square.

The futures strip is a different animal. Real-time CME futures data costs real money, and no free feed carries it. So ES, NQ, YM, oil, and gold up there are the genuine contracts, delayed roughly fifteen minutes. They answer "what's the overnight mood" and "is the market open somewhere doing something" — they do not answer "what is the S&P doing this second." The VIX is an index, not a future, and comes on the same delay.

News comes from Finnhub: a general market wire plus stories tagged to each of your holdings from the last week. Analyst ratings, price targets, and next-earnings dates come from Yahoo's consensus data and refresh about every half hour, which is plenty — analysts don't change their minds by the minute, whatever the headlines imply.

Everything is cached at a sane rhythm: prices every ~2s, futures 20s, the analysis engine 45s, news 3 minutes, fundamentals an hour. If a source dies, its panel goes quiet and everything else keeps running. A dashboard that falls over because one API hiccuped is a dashboard you'll stop trusting.

Reading the charts the way they're drawn

Each holding card draws a year of daily closes. The bright line is price. The amber line is the 20-day simple moving average; the grey one is the 50-day. A moving average is exactly what it says: the plain average of the last N closes, dragged forward one day at a time. That's all. Its whole job is to be slower than price — a memory the market has to fight against.

Price above a rising average means the recent past keeps getting repriced upward; below a falling one, the opposite. When the 20 crosses above the 50 people call it a golden cross; the reverse, a death cross. The names oversell it. A crossover is a lagging summary of what already happened — useful for describing a trend, expensive if you treat it as a starting gun, because by the time the slow line agrees, a chunk of the move is gone. You'll see exactly how expensive in the Strategy Lab.

The little sparklines in the holdings table are the same year compressed to a gesture — green if it ends higher than it started, red otherwise. They're there for shape recognition, nothing more.

The per-stock numbers, one at a time

RSI — how one-sided has the tape been

RSI answers a single question: of all the daily movement in the last 14 sessions, what share of it was upward? Everything up: 100. Everything down: 0. An even split: 50. Wilder's smoothing makes recent days count a bit more, and that's genuinely the whole machine.

The folklore says 70 is "overbought" and 30 is "oversold," as if those were laws. They're habits. A strong stock can sit above 70 for months while the pullback you're waiting for never arrives. Read RSI as a description of how lopsided the recent tape has been — and notice that extreme readings in either direction mean the move is stretched, which cuts both ways.

Wilder smoothing: each day, new_avg = (13 × old_avg + today) / 14 — an exponential average with a slow decay, so a monster day fades over weeks instead of dropping off a cliff on day 15.

MACD — is the trend speeding up or tiring

Take a fast exponential average of price (12 days), subtract a slow one (26): that difference is momentum with the units stripped away. Smooth that (9 days) and look at whether the gap between the two is widening or shrinking. The dashboard reduces the whole apparatus to one honest word: rising — trend gaining energy — or fading. It's a second derivative in a trench coat. Treat it as texture, not signal.

Momentum — 5, 20, 60 days

Plain percentage change over roughly a week, a month, and a quarter. No smoothing, no cleverness. Three windows because one lies: a stock can be up on the month and bleeding on the week, and you want to see both at once.

Volatility — the price of holding it

Take daily log-returns for the last month, measure how much they scatter, scale to a year. The result reads like "29%/yr" and means: if the recent behavior kept up, a one-standard-deviation year moves the price about that much, either direction. The rough day-to-day translation is annualized ÷ 16 — a 32% stock wobbles about 2% on an ordinary day, before anything actually happens.

The card shows the EWMA version — RiskMetrics-style, recent days weighted heavier — so when a name suddenly turns violent, the number turns violent with it instead of averaging the storm away. That's also why your book's "current regime" volatility and its "1-year average" volatility can disagree: when current is higher, the recent market is rougher than the year as a whole.

ATR, Bollinger %b — the same idea in price units

ATR is the average true daily range in dollars — what a normal day's swing physically looks like — useful for sizing what "noise" means for a given name. Bollinger %b asks where price sits inside a two-standard-deviation envelope of its last 20 days: 0.5 is mid-band and calm; hugging 0 or 1 means stretched relative to its own recent self. Stretched is a state, not a verdict — rubber bands snap back, and they also break.

Relative strength vs SPY — the only fair fight

Your stock being up 4% in a month means one thing if the index did 1% and something much worse if the index did 9%. The vs-SPY figure is simply your holding's return minus SPY's over the same window. Positive: it's earning its place. Persistently negative: you're paying opportunity cost to hold a laggard, and the market is patient about collecting.

Max drawdown, Sharpe, 52-week position

Max drawdown is the deepest peak-to-trough fall in the window — the worst experience an owner actually had, and the number that best predicts whether you'll hold through it, because you already know how the last one felt. Sharpe is annualized return divided by annualized volatility: pain-adjusted performance. Above 1 is good, negative means you took risk and got punished for it. And the 52-week position is just where price sits between its yearly low (0%) and high (100%) — cheap-looking near the bottom is sometimes cheap and sometimes broken, and the number can't tell you which.

The conditions score — a sort key wearing a suit

Every holding card carries a 0–100 score. Here is everything it is: five sub-scores — trend, momentum, strength vs SPY, risk, and stretch — each computed from rules you can read in analysis.py, blended with fixed weights (25 / 25 / 20 / 15 / 15). The sub-scores are printed right under the bar so you can see why a number is what it is, not just what it is.

Here is everything it is not: a prediction, a recommendation, or a substitute for reading the card. Compressing five dimensions into one number loses information on purpose — that's what makes it useful for sorting and dangerous for deciding. High score means "conditions currently look orderly by these five lenses." Markets take special pleasure in punishing orderly.

Portfolio risk — the panel that earns the rent

Everything above describes single names. This panel describes the thing you actually own: the combination. And combinations behave in ways the pieces don't advertise.

Effective holdings — how many bets is this, really

You hold — positions. You do not hold — bets. Square each position's weight, add them up, take the reciprocal — that's the Herfindahl trick — and you get the number of equal-sized positions your book behaves like. Yours right now: —. The gap between those two numbers is concentration, and concentration is a decision you're making whether or not you made it on purpose.

Weight vs risk contribution — where the money is vs what moves it

The paired bars are the panel's centerpiece. The violet bar is a position's share of your dollars. The amber bar is its share of your risk — computed from the covariance matrix, so it accounts for both how violent a name is and how much it drags the others with it. They are almost never the same number. — A small position in something wild can quietly matter more than a large position in something sleepy. This is the math that makes that visible.

Beta and the diversification ratio

Beta regresses your whole book against SPY: at 1.0 you're a passenger on the index, above it you're the index with the volume turned up, below it you march to something else. Yours: —. A low beta is not the same as low risk — it can also mean your risk is idiosyncratic, concentrated in stories the index doesn't share. The diversification ratio (—×) says how much cancellation you're getting: it's the weighted average of your holdings' individual volatilities divided by the portfolio's actual volatility. At 1.0, nothing offsets anything.

VaR and CVaR — a bad day, in dollars

Value-at-Risk is a sentence pretending to be a number. The sentence: "on 19 days out of 20, you won't lose more than this." The dashboard computes it two ways on purpose. The bell-curve version assumes returns are normally distributed. The historical version doesn't assume anything — it sorts the days your book actually lived through and reads the 5th percentile straight off the record. When historical is uglier than the bell curve (—), your returns have fat tails: finance-speak for "the bad days are worse than a bell curve promises," which is true of almost everything worth owning.

VaR's famous flaw is that it says nothing about the day you blow through it. That's CVaR — the average of the tail. Not "how bad is the line," but "when the line breaks, how bad is it out there." The workbench, model by model

Reverse DCF — the only honest DCF

A normal DCF asks you to guess growth for ten years, then pretends the resulting "fair value" is a discovery instead of an echo of your guess. This one runs the machine backwards: it takes the price as given and solves for the growth rate that would justify it. Then it puts that number next to what the company has actually grown free cash flow at, and leaves the room. The judgment — "is the implied number believable?" — is yours, because that judgment is the entire investment decision and no model should be allowed to launder it for you.

The sensitivity grid exists because the answer is hostage to two assumptions: the discount rate (what return you demand for tying up money in this) and terminal growth (what the business does forever after year ten). Small changes in either move the implied growth a lot. That's not a flaw in the grid — that's the grid telling you how soft the ground is under every DCF you've ever seen.

Comps — priced against the neighbors

Multiples are shorthand: P/E is years of current earnings you're paying for; EV/EBITDA is the same idea with debt counted and accounting noise reduced; FCF yield is the cash the business throws off per dollar of price. The comps tab pulls a live peer list, computes the medians (excluding "nm" garbage — negative or absurd multiples, the way any comps sheet would), and shows what your company would be worth if the market paid it the peer-median multiple.

That "if" is load-bearing. Persistent gaps usually have reasons — margin, growth, backlog quality, balance sheet — and a peer list is an opinion wearing a spreadsheet. When the implied values sprawl, the tab says so out loud, because the honest reading of a wide comps range is "this peer set is telling you about itself."

3-Statement — where accounting meets cash

Three views of one machine. The income statement is the story: revenue in, costs out, profit "earned" — in quotes because it's an opinion shaped by accrual rules. The balance sheet is the photograph: what's owned and owed at one instant. The cash flow statement is the polygraph: what actually moved through the bank account. The line the tab draws for you — net income becoming operating cash becoming free cash flow after capex — is the single most underrated check in investing. Profits are a claim. Cash is a fact. Companies where the claim and the fact diverge for years in a row are telling you something, rarely something good.

LBO — the house-flipper's trick, priced

Every leveraged buyout is a mortgage story. Buy the asset with a slice of your own money and a pile of borrowed money; let the asset's own cash flow pay the loan down; sell. All the appreciation — and all the paydown — lands on your slice. The tab prices whether that trick works here, at this price: entry premium, leverage, cost of debt, EBITDA growth, exit multiple, all sliders. The verdict is an IRR next to the ~20% a sponsor would demand. When it can't get there at the defaults, the model isn't saying the company is bad — it's saying the market already charged you for the good news.

M&A — accretion is bookkeeping, not value

The accretion/dilution tab does the arithmetic a banker does in the first hour: new shares issued for the stock portion, after-tax interest on the borrowed cash portion, two income streams combined, synergies if you're feeling generous — does earnings-per-share go up or down? It matters because boards and markets react to it. But hold the thought loosely: EPS accretion is a bookkeeping outcome. Overpaying with expensive stock can look "accretive" while torching value; a brilliant deal can screen dilutive in year one. The tab hands you the arithmetic frame and the breakeven synergies number. The verdict it deliberately doesn't hand you.

Monte Carlo — a thousand rhymes, no forecast

Take your book's actual daily returns — the real joint history, so the correlations and the fat tails come along for free. Shuffle days with replacement into two thousand alternate years. Stack the endings into a distribution. That's the whole method, and it's called bootstrapping: no bell curve assumed anywhere, seeded so you get identical output on every run.

Read the cone as "the range of futures consistent with the past repeating in a different order" — nothing more. It cannot see a regime change, a war, a product failure. What it's genuinely good for is calibrating your stomach: if the 5th-percentile ending, in dollars, is a number you can't live with, the time to know that is now, while it's hypothetical.

Stress — the rear-view mirror, used honestly

The stress tab replays named storms — the COVID crash, the 2022 rate shock, SVB week, the August-24 vol spike — against today's weights, and shows SPY beside your book for scale. Where a holding didn't exist yet, it says so and renormalizes instead of quietly pretending. The next storm will be different; that's not the point. The point is a floor of realism under "how bad can a bad month look," from months that actually happened.

Strategy Lab — watching the machines lose

The lab backtests three famous mechanical strategies against buy-and-hold: the 50/200 moving-average cross (trend following), 12-month momentum with the last month skipped (the academic classic), and RSI(2) mean-reversion (buy panic, sell relief). Signals execute the next day, every position flip pays five basis points, and the table shows time-in-market — because a strategy that's in cash half the time and merely ties buy-and-hold is quietly winning on risk.

And a warning that belongs in bold anywhere backtests appear: every backtest overfits by existence. These strategies are famous because they worked in the past — that's survivorship in the idea market. Real costs run worse than five basis points. The past happened once; the future samples from a wider set. The lab exists so you can watch the mechanics — see the trend filter skip a crash and then miss the rebound — not so you can pick a winner. If one of these tables ever convinces you to trade, reread this paragraph first.

Where all of this lies to you

A tools page that doesn't disclose its failure modes is a sales page. So: prices are IEX, a thin slice of the tape. Futures run fifteen minutes behind. Yahoo's fundamentals are mostly right and occasionally stale or oddly mapped, which is why the workbench echoes its inputs — check them against a filing before betting on a model output. Analyst targets are opinions with a famous optimism bias. Volatility, correlation, beta are all computed from a window, and windows end; correlations especially have a habit of converging on 1.0 during exactly the crises you diversified for. Every risk number here describes the past. The market is under no obligation to resemble it.

And the one that matters most: nothing on this dashboard knows about your income, your taxes, your timeline, or what losing this money would actually do to your life. That context is the majority of every real decision, and it lives with you, not in the code.
